Lexsi Labs Research · AI Safety and Alignment Team · September 2026

When we open-sourced the Alignment and Safety Stack, we made a claim that needs to be demonstrated, not asserted: that some bounded failures live in the learned behaviour of the model, and can be reached, corrected and re-verified there. This post is our first demonstration. We ran the full loop end to end on two audited failures and produced two corrected checkpoints.

We call the method targeted model repair: a controlled offline update to an existing checkpoint, evaluated against a specification of what must change and what must remain intact. It borrows from change management. Identify and scope the defect, apply a bounded correction, regression-test the candidate, version the result, and keep the original as a rollback point. A weight edit counts as a repair only when the resulting checkpoint satisfies that specification; touching few parameters does not by itself imply a small behavioural blast radius.[1]

Why this matters now An organisation invests in integration, latency, security review and validation before a model reaches production. When one behaviour fails, the goal is to fix that behaviour without reopening everything already tested.

We chose the two settings deliberately. Multilingual safety is the clearer case and the one that matters most for sovereign and regional deployments: the failure sits in particular languages of a checkpoint that is otherwise useful and broadly capable. Cybersecurity is the harder, more universal case: an exploit and a hardening guide draw on the same knowledge, so suppressing the attacker's version risks suppressing the defender's. Running both lets us test whether the workflow holds beyond one failure type, and see exactly where selectivity breaks down.

If you run a frontier lab

Two bounded weight edits on a released checkpoint, with the measured collateral effects reported alongside the gains

No broad retraining: the checkpoint is corrected offline using curated correction data. Selected MLP weights of google/gemma-4-31B-it change, and each candidate is scored on 41 and 47 held-out rows against a specification written before the edit. The multilingual edit moves all nine harmful-refusal tests in the audit the safer way; the cyber edit cuts attacker-useful output on every single-turn attacker benchmark, by up to 4.4 times on FORTRESS, and holds ten defensive tasks at the same actionability grade. Both edits, built on different data, let 2.6 times fewer five-turn jailbreaks through.

What we would ask next in your place: edit size, compute, a fine-tuning baseline on the same data, and transfer beyond one family. Those are the next programme, and the fairness shift on the multilingual edit, which the cyber edit does not produce, is the first thing on it.

If you deploy models in a regulated business

A corrected checkpoint designed for the deployment path you already validated

The repaired checkpoint has the same tokenizer, the same configuration and the same serving path as the base; only selected weights change. It arrives with a validation pack: what improved, what held within a point, what regressed, and a pre-specified acceptance gate your model-risk function can run against your own workload. No wrapper, no router, nothing new in the request path.

1. Capable models are not aligned model

Before repairing anything, we audited seven open-weight models of similar scale under one fixed protocol: the same datasets, the same prompts, the same judges, the same token budgets, and no combining of rows into a single safety score. The set spans the current global open families (Gemma 3 and 4, Qwen 3.5, Ministral 3) and three sovereign models built for Indian languages (Sarvam, Param-2, Gnani-Evon). Every model in it is capable. Several lead a benchmark. None of them is safe across the board, and the pattern of where each one fails is the case for correction.[1, Table 1]

Why one protocolPublished safety numbers are not comparable across model cards: different prompt sets, different judges, different generation lengths. Under a shared protocol the differences that remain are in the models. Each row below is a separate test; the paper never ranks them into one score.

The risk map below is the whole audit on one page. Five of its thirteen tests carry the argument and follow as cards: each ranks the seven models, marks the weakest, and explains what the test measures and what a poor result means in production. The other eight are one click further down. The point of reading them in sequence is that a model which looks safe on the first test is usually exposed on the fourth or the seventh.

The sovereign angleSovereign models are trained to serve regional languages and culture, and on native comprehension they do. Safety is usually the smallest line in that budget, measured once at release in the highest-resource language and assumed to transfer. Charts 07 to 10 show how far it does.

Key risks the audit exposes
1
The strongest refusers are the least usable. Qwen3.5 and Param-2 are perfect on HarmBench and are the two weakest on OR-Bench-Hard, refusing 89% and 66% of hard benign requests.
2
Safety does not travel with language. Every model scores 39 to 61 points lower on the 23-language Indic set than on English HarmBench. Sarvam, built for these languages, is the weakest on it.
3
Single-turn scores overstate protection. Three of seven refuse fewer than half of five-turn jailbreaks; the repair target holds 48.6. Hedged answers leak substance past a refusal on StrongREJECT.
4
Capability and hazard sit together. The most capable model in the set, Gemma-4, holds the most hazardous cyber knowledge (75.7 on WMDP-cyber). Knowledge is not willingness; it cannot be refused away.
5
No model is a safe default. Gemma-3 is best on usability and weakest on bias and professional harm. Gemma-4, the strongest on capability, is best on three rows and weakest only on hazardous knowledge. Swapping models moves the gap; it does not close it.

01.H

What this audit does not settle

An audit is only as good as its gaps are visible. These are the ones the report records, and they bound what the charts above can claim.

Benchmarks inform gates; they do not set them

A hard benign set can reveal a regression without estimating how often it occurs in a given application. Deployment acceptance needs a named workload, representative tests and tolerances fixed before the approval test. None of the numbers above is a pass mark.

Two models reason whether asked to or not

Sarvam-30B and Param-2-17B cannot switch off reasoning. Unfinished answers count as non-refusal, which depresses Sarvam on SORRY-Bench, MultiJail and WMDP, and Param-2 often selects option D on multiple choice. Their rows are reported under the shared protocol and flagged where it matters.

Several scores had to be repaired before they could be trusted

The companion audit found repetitive output scored as safe, a guard classifier that missed most unsafe replies, an inverted label scheme and a tokeniser mismatch that emptied generations. Each let a score overstate protection. The India suite's default judge agrees poorly with stronger judges on Indic responses, so a three-judge panel is used there.

Per-language benign refusal and script fidelity are not reported

The report measures over-refusal on English sets and IndicSafe's benign arm in aggregate. Whether a model refuses legitimate requests in each Indic language, and whether it answers in the right script, are listed as not reported in the acceptance ledger, and they decide deployability for a multilingual use case.

Some sets have no control arm

BanglaSafe has no benign condition, so the row shows refusal without its over-refusal cost. Sarvam's Indic-Bias value covers only the 1,102 answers it parsed. WMDP is left unranked because unfinished reasoning affects two models.

The audit stops at the model

Everything here is a single model answering a prompt. Tool use, agent loops and retrieval are outside the protocol. A model that holds on these tests can still fail when it is given a shell, a browser or a document store, and that setting is where the cybersecurity repair matters most.

For an institution putting one of these models into mission-critical work, the deploying organisation owns the risk. When the goal is to correct the behaviour inside the checkpoint rather than add another serving-time control, the safety programme has to reach the learned behaviour itself. That is what makes model correction the outcome that matters, not just another benchmark.

02

2. What a repair actually is, and the stack it runs on

We start from a concrete finding, an audit result or a production incident, and convert it into an acceptance specification: where the failure occurs, what behaviour should replace it, what surrounding behaviour the deployment depends on, and what evidence decides whether the correction is acceptable. Then the correction is a bounded offline weight update. The output is one static checkpoint, with no per-language router, no runtime steering hook and no added serving component.

A target metric is not enough A model that refuses more can simply be a model that refuses everything. Every repair is scored on three axes at once: harmful refusal, over-refusal on hard benign requests, and the utility the deployment relies on.

02.3

Eight properties of the method, each with its evidence

What the stack commits to on every repair, and where in this post or the release post the evidence sits. None of it is specific to Gemma.

  1. ✓
    Correction in the weightsOne replacement checkpoint. No router, steering hook or policy layer; same tokenizer, standard engines, no additional permanent serving-time component.
  2. ✓
    Specification before interventionTarget, preserve, watch and decision rule written first. Held-out sets never overlap the correction data.
  3. ✓
    Edit bounded to part of the checkpointCircuit-guided restriction keeps the update bounded to a selected part of the checkpoint. Selected MLP weights here.
  4. ✓
    Three axes, never one numberHarmful refusal, benign over-refusal and utility on every card. Steering matched the refusal gain at 68.0% benign refused; the edit held 2.8%.
  5. ✓
    Content judged, not the opening lineWhole-response judge, an independent second judge, and an actionability grade from executable to flat refusal.
  6. ✓
    Interpretability held to a testA circuit explanation counts only if the intervention it selects improves the model. Faithful but unactionable is flagged.
  7. ✓

    Evidence travels with the checkpointProvenance per sample, one comparable audit record, every held-out row published. Rejection is a recorded outcome.

03 · REPAIR ONE

3. Multilingual safety: one checkpoint, six languages, every safety test moves the safer way

The audit finding: google/gemma-4-31B-it refuses harmful requests inconsistently across English and five Indic languages. The objective: raise aggregate harmful-request refusal across all six. The constraint: one checkpoint, no per-language router, and no loss of benign assistance, script fidelity or broad capability.

How we corrected it. We built correction data of two kinds: harmful requests in each language that expose the failure, and legitimate requests that define the assistance to keep, with labels, coverage and language quality checked and the sets held apart from every evaluation set. From that data we made a bounded offline update to selected MLP weights of the checkpoint, one correction per language, then consolidated the corrections into a single checkpoint and evaluated that checkpoint from scratch, because corrections can interact. The intervention method, the circuit tooling that scopes the update and the curation pipeline are described in the companion papers. No retraining, no runtime router, no language detection at serving time; the tokenizer and configuration are unchanged.[3, 4, 5]

A check the target implies A multilingual repair must not make the model answer in the wrong language or emit malformed text. What holds today: language identification across 22 Indic languages is unchanged (97.4 → 97.2 native script, 86.1 → 86.5 romanised), and Indic reading comprehension holds across 11 languages (IndicQA 73.4 → 73.2). Per-language output-script fidelity, the share of answers written in the expected native script, is the next gate for this checkpoint.

04 · REPAIR TWO

4. Cybersecurity: making a model refuse to help attackers

This is the more universal of the two repairs and the one with the larger consequence. The moment a model is given tools, a shell, a browser or a document store, the question stops being whether it will write an exploit when asked. It becomes whether, somewhere inside a long task, it will enumerate permissions it was not asked to enumerate, or help a user who has split an attack into steps that each look routine.

The problem this repair addresses is narrower and measurable: models can provide attacker-useful assistance across single- and multi-turn interactions, including when an attack is decomposed across turns so that each request looks routine. A refusal classifier that keys on the opening sentence passes an answer that says no and then supplies the payload. The behaviour has to be corrected where it lives, in the weights, and then re-verified after any deployment transform.

The intent of this repairRemove the model's willingness to take attacker-useful steps, so that it adheres to the specification of the system it operates in, while keeping the defensive and engineering assistance that draws on the same knowledge. Knowledge stays; willingness changes. An agent can inherit its model's willingness. This repair corrects that willingness at the chat level, single- and multi-turn; agentic and prompt-injection evaluations are the next step.

Why this is the hard case. In multilingual safety the harmful and the legitimate rarely look alike. In cybersecurity they share the same terminology, the same systems and the same technical detail: an exploit and a hardening guide are near-twins, and a dangerous question and a routine one in a security operations centre use the same words. Suppressing attacker-useful assistance therefore risks suppressing the defender's version, and a bare refusal rate cannot tell you which you got. The correction is built the same way as the multilingual one, a bounded offline update to selected MLP weights on different correction data, and then scored two-sidedly: attacker-useful content on one side, legitimate assistance on the other, never folded into one number.[1, §4.2]

A refusal is not a blockThe base model often opens with “I cannot help with that” and then supplies the IMDS endpoint, the token sequence and the enumeration workflow. Every attacker-useful row below scores the content for usable detail whether or not it starts with a refusal: a GPT-4o-mini judge reads the whole response, with the open WildGuard classifier as an independent second opinion. A refusal detector that keys on the opening sentence, as many simple guardrails do, passes the hedged answer.

5. What makes a repair deployable

Both repairs moved the audited behaviour in the intended direction and left broad capability largely intact. Neither is certified by that. A candidate must improve the target, preserve the required behaviour, and keep each documented cost within the tolerance of a named use case. We publish the candidate and its full ledger; the owner of the application makes the deployment decision from that evidence, against tests they fixed before running them.

For a regulated deployer this is a familiar object. A repaired checkpoint is a new model version with a validation pack. It enters the same change-management process as any model change: a stated scope (the target behaviour), a regression suite (the held rows), a list of known deviations (the regressed rows), a pre-specified acceptance test on the production workload, and the original checkpoint retained as the rollback point. Because the tokenizer, configuration and serving path are unchanged, the serving architecture does not gain a new permanent component, so the existing integration path largely remains intact; the replacement checkpoint still requires behavioural and workload-specific validation, and the ledger says exactly which rows that validation must cover.

Rejection is a valid resultWhen a candidate moves the target metric but exceeds the deployment’s tolerance elsewhere, the right outcome is to revise or reject it. That decision is only possible because the cost was measured, not hidden.

05.1

Six practices for making a model safe, for agentic work in particular

Six practices both repairs relied on, each visible in the rows above. None of them is specific to Gemma, to one failure type, or to Lexsi tooling; they are what we would ask of any safety claim, including our own.

Score the content, not the opening sentence.

A refusal phrase followed by the payload is a failure. Judge the whole response for attacker-useful detail, with a second independent judge.

Test the conversation, not the prompt.

Multi-turn and decomposed requests are the agentic failure mode. In our view, conversation-level attack success is a closer guide to behaviour in a tool loop than a single-prompt score.

Measure legitimate work next to the harm.

An exploit and a hardening guide share vocabulary. Benign-refusal sets built from real security questions are the only way to see the cost of a correction.

Change willingness, keep knowledge.

Hazardous-knowledge accuracy should move little. A correction that erases knowledge damages the defensive uses that draw on the same facts.

Hold the measurement fixed.

Same prompt format, generation length, judge and scoring rules for base and repair. A judge mismatch can manufacture an improvement.

Name the workload before approving.

Benchmarks locate the cost; they do not set the tolerance. Acceptance needs representative benign and harmful tests for the application, fixed before the test is run.

7. Working with us

The loop in this post is the engagement. It runs the same way for a lab releasing weights and for an institution deploying them; what differs is who names the failure and who owns the acceptance test.

For frontier and sovereign model labs

A second, independent safety pass on a checkpoint you are about to release or have already shipped, with the correction returned as a candidate you can evaluate, extend or reject.

  1. You bring the checkpoint and the finding.Weights only; no training data is required. The finding can be an internal audit, a red-team result, or ours.
  2. We write the specification with you.Target behaviour, preserved behaviour, the held-out sets, and the gates, fixed before any edit is made.
  3. We return a candidate and its ledger.A versioned checkpoint with every held-out row, improved, held and regressed, under a shared protocol you can rerun.
  4. You decide, and we iterate.Revise against the same specification, or extend the correction to further failures, languages or domains.

For regulated deployers

A corrected checkpoint designed for the deployment path you already validated, with the evidence your model-risk function needs to approve the change.

  1. You name the workload.The application, the languages, the user groups, and a representative set of benign and harmful requests from it.
  2. You fix the tolerances.How much benign refusal the workload can absorb, which behaviours must not move, and the capability rows that matter, before the test is run.
  3. We return a candidate and its validation pack.Same tokenizer, configuration and serving path as your base; the ledger maps to your change-management record. For a multilingual deployment, the pack includes benign refusal and native-script fidelity for each of your languages.
  4. Your team runs the acceptance test.Against your own prompts and your own gates. The original checkpoint remains the rollback point.

Both checkpoints in this post are on Hugging Face behind a gated form, with the tooling in the Lexsi Labs GitHub organisation. For a conversation about a checkpoint of yours: research@lexsi.ai.
